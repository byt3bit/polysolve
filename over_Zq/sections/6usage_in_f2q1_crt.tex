In previous sections we presented our algorithm for solving the LWE problem over $\Z_q$ and its variants. The algorithm is based on the construction of a Gr\"obner basis for the ideal generated by the polynomial system corresponding to the LWE instance. In this section we discuss how to solve the problem by decomposing it into multiple polynomial system solving instances over finite fields.

\textbf{Mapping to finite fields.} Consider the ring \(\Z_q\) where \(q\) is a composite integer. 
We can factor \(q\) into its prime power factors as \(q = \prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{e_i}\). By the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT), we have an isomorphism:
\[\Z_{q} \cong \prod_{i=1}^r \Z_{p_i^{e_i}}.\]
This means that we can represent elements of \(\Z_{q}\) as tuples of elements in the rings \(\Z_{p_i^{e_i}}\). In particular, we can represent the LWE samples as tuples of samples in the rings \(\Z_{p_i^{e_i}}\). This allows us to apply our algorithm to each component of the tuple separately, effectively solving the LWE problem over the finite fields \(\F_{p_i}\) for each prime factor \(p_i\) of \(q\). By exploiting Hensel's lifting, we can lift the solutions obtained in the finite fields to obtain a solution in the corresponding extension fields \(\F_{p_i^{e_i}}\) and then combine the solutions using the CRT to obtain a solution in \(\Z_{q}\).

\noindent
\textbf{Practical considerations.} While in theory this approach permits to avoid scalar multiplication in the $S$-polynomial computation (the diagonalization would generate monic polynomials), in practice it may not be more efficient than working directly over \(\Z_q\), due to the overhead of the mappings and the fact that the algorithm's complexity would depend on the number of elements in the prime factorization of $q$. Therefore, it is important to carefully analyze the trade-offs and consider the specific parameters of the problem when deciding whether to use this approach. As a result, this consideration could help in identifying possibly weak instances.
Note that when working over \(\F_{p_i}\), the algorithm can be slightly more efficient. In particular, the running time of the algorithm (not the asymptotic complexity) is improved by replacing the Smith Normal Form with the Reduced Row Echelon Form which leads to monic polynomials and this avoids the need of scalar multiplications in the $S$-polynomial computation and reduction. 
Considering the complexity of the final CRT, given by \(\bigO(\log_2^2(q))\)~\cite{DBLP:books/daglib/0018101}, an advantage of this approach arises when $q = \prod_{i=1}^r p_i$ and 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(r\) is small enough to justify the multiple application of the algorithm: the repeated application of the algorithm is less computational expensive than performing all the scalar multiplications over \(\Z_q\);
    \item for \(1 \le i \le r\), \(p_i << q\) (e.g. $q$ is smooth): this allows efficient computations in the finite fields \(\F_{p_i}\).
\end{itemize}

When the exponents \(e_i\) in the prime factorization of \(q\) are greater than 1, the lifting step may introduce additional overhead and complexity which could become a bottleneck in the overall algorithm. In this case, a better approach could be to directly work over the rings \(\Z_{p_i^{e_i}}\). If \(\sum_{i=1}^r \log_2(p_i^{e_i}) << \log_2(q)\), due to the smaller size of the rings \(\Z_{p_i^{e_i}}\) compared to \(\Z_q\),repeating the algorithm $r$ times could be more efficient than working directly over \(\Z_q\).
